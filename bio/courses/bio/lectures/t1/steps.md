### Karyology & karyotype
- **Karyology** = branch of cytology dealing with the structure of cell nuclei, especially chromosomes (p. 5)
- **Karyotype** = the number and appearance of chromosomes in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell (p. 5)
- Chromosomes first observed in plant cells by **Carl Wilhelm von Nägeli (1842)**; their behaviour in salamander cells described by **Walther Flemming (1882)**, discoverer of mitosis (p. 6)
- DNA is packed into **chromatin** with the help of **histone** proteins (p. 7)
- ↳ **Euchromatin**: transcriptionally active DNA
- ↳ **Heterochromatin**: very dense DNA, transcriptionally inactive


### Observations on karyotypes
1. **Differences in chromosome number** (p. 8–10)
- ↳ Somatic cells = **2n**; gametes = **n** (mitosis keeps 2n, meiosis halves to n)
- ↳ **Monoploid number (x)** = number of chromosomes in the ancestral (non-homologous) set; may or may not equal the haploid number n. E.g. 2n = 2x = 20 (x = 10) vs 2n = 4x = 20 (x = 5) (p. 9)
- ↳ Lowest somatic number: ***Haplopappus gracilis*, 2n = 4** (p. 10)
- ↳ Highest: ferns, ***Ophioglossum reticulatum*, 2n > 1200** (p. 10)
2. **Differences in absolute chromosome size**: up to ~20-fold between genera of one family (p. 11)
- ↳ Small: ~0.2 µm in **Cyperaceae** (limit of light-microscope resolution)
- ↳ Big: **Paeoniaceae, Liliaceae**; some *Lilium* chromosomes reach **25 µm**
3. **Differences in chromosome shape**: position of the **centromere**, probably due to translocations (p. 12)
- ↳ Telocentric → Acrocentric → Submetacentric → Metacentric
4. **Chromosome mutations** (p. 13)
- ↳ Single-chromosome: **deletion, duplication, inversion**
- ↳ Two-chromosome: **insertion, translocation**

## Nuclear DNA amount (genome size) · Polyploidy & aneuploidy | 4 min
- Expressed as **base pairs** or **weight in picograms (pg)**; **1 pg DNA = 978 Mbp** (p. 14)
- **C-value** = amount of DNA in a haploid nucleus (gamete) = half the amount in a diploid somatic cell (p. 14)
- Plants vary immensely: smallest ***Genlisea aurea* 0.065 pg/1C**, largest ***Paris japonica* 152.23 pg/1C** (p. 14)
- Genome size ranges by group (chart): flowering plants and amphibians have the widest ranges; birds and mammals are narrow (p. 15)
- **C-value enigma / paradox**: genome size does **not correlate with organismal complexity**; e.g. some single-celled protists have far larger genomes than humans. Table: *Fritillaria assyriaca* (lily) 127.4 pg, frog 5.6–8.0 pg, human 3.5 pg, mouse 3.4 pg, *E. coli* 0.0046 pg; large genomes have a high % of transposable elements (TEs) (p. 16)
- **Prokaryotes have no introns**. Intron = nucleotide sequence within a gene that is removed by RNA splicing during maturation of the RNA (p. 17)


### Polyploidy & aneuploidy
- **Polyploid** = containing more than two paired (homologous) sets of chromosomes (n, 2n, 4n, 6n…) (p. 18)
- **Aneuploidy** = abnormal number of chromosomes in a cell (p. 19)
- ↳ **Hypoploidy**: **monosomy (2n−1)**, one chromosome of a homologous pair missing; **nullisomy (2n−2)**, a whole homologous pair lost
- ↳ **Hyperploidy**: **trisomy (2n+1)**, **tetrasomy (2n+2)**, three or four copies of a chromosome instead of two
- **Plants vs animals** (p. 20–22)
- ↳ In plants aneuploidy has **no significant consequences**; mono-/nullisomics (if they survive) have lower vitality and fertility; extra chromosomes may have no effect. Example: nullisomic lines of *Triticum aestivum* (wheat) (p. 21)
- ↳ In animals: loss of > **5 %** of genome or excess > **10 %** is **always lethal**; smaller changes are viable but with malformations (lowered intelligence, fertility). Example: **Down syndrome** (trisomy 21) (p. 20, p. 22)
- **Autopolyploidy** = duplication of the genome of one species. Examples: *Dactylis polygama* 2n = 2x = 14 → *D. glomerata* 2n = 4x = 28; *Alnus glutinosa* 2n = 2x = 28 → *A. rohlenae* 2n = 4x = 56 (p. 23–25)
- **Allopolyploidy** = hybridisation of two different species + genome doubling. Example: *Galeopsis pubescens* (2x = 16) × *G. speciosa* (2x = 16) → ***G. tetrahit* 2n = 4x = 32** (p. 23, p. 26)
- **Majority of living biota are polyploids**; of the world's 21 major crops, **83.7 % of production comes from polyploids** (wheat, rice, maize, potato, sugar cane…) vs 16.3 % diploids (barley, millet, beans…) (p. 27)

## DNA | 3 min
- **DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)** = two nucleotide chains coiled into a **double helix**, carrying genetic instructions for all known organisms and many viruses (p. 28)
- Located in the **nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts** (p. 28)
- One human cell's DNA is **> 4 m** long, packed in a nucleus ~5 µm (five thousandths of a mm) in diameter (p. 28)
- Nucleic acids are one of the **four major macromolecules** (with proteins, lipids and polysaccharides) (p. 28)
- Typing the human genome at 60 wpm, 8 h/day would take ~50 years (p. 28)
- History (p. 29–30): structure identified by **James Watson & Francis Crick** (Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge), **Nature, 25 April 1953**; guided by X-ray diffraction data from **Raymond Gosling**, PhD student of **Rosalind Franklin**, who used X-ray crystallography and first showed the spiral structure. **Nobel Prize 1962** (Physiology or Medicine): **Crick, Watson and Maurice Wilkins**
- **Nucleotide** = (1) nitrogenous base + (2) sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA) + (3) phosphate group (p. 32–34)
- ↳ **Purines**: adenine (A), guanine (G)
- ↳ **Pyrimidines**: cytosine (C), thymine (T, DNA only), uracil (U, RNA only)
- **Phosphodiester bond**: links nucleotides on the same strand, between the **3′-OH** of one sugar and the **5′-phosphate** of the next; strands have a 5′ end and a 3′ end (p. 35)
- **Complementary base pairing**: purine pairs with pyrimidine (p. 36–37)
- ↳ **A–T: 2 hydrogen bonds** (A–U in RNA, also 2)
- ↳ **C–G: 3 hydrogen bonds**
- Double helix stabilised by **hydrogen bonds between bases** and **base-stacking interactions** among aromatic bases (p. 38)

## RNA | 2 min
- DNA is in the nucleus; RNA in the **cytoplasm and nucleolus** (p. 31)
- RNA differs from DNA in 3 ways (p. 39):
- ↳ usually **single-stranded** and much shorter
- ↳ sugar = **ribose** (not deoxyribose)
- ↳ **uracil** instead of thymine (uracil = unmethylated form of thymine)
- **Types of RNA** (p. 40):
- ↳ **mRNA** (messenger): carries information on the protein sequence to ribosomes
- ↳ **tRNA** (transfer): brings a specific amino acid to the growing polypeptide at the ribosome during translation
- ↳ **rRNA** (ribosomal): combines with protein in the cytoplasm to form the **ribosome** (a nucleoprotein)
- **Central dogma** (p. 41): DNA → (replication) DNA; DNA → (**transcription**) RNA → (**translation**) protein; RNA → DNA = **reverse ("back") transcription**; RNA can also replicate

## Processes step by step | 2 min
### Central dogma (p. 41)
1. **Replication**: DNA → DNA
2. **Transcription**: DNA → RNA
3. **Translation**: RNA → protein
4. **Reverse transcription**: RNA → DNA (and RNA replication)

### Allopolyploid formation (p. 23, p. 26)
1. Two different diploid species cross (e.g. *Galeopsis pubescens* × *G. speciosa*, both 2n = 16)
2. The hybrid genome doubles
3. A fertile tetraploid arises (*G. tetrahit*, 2n = 4x = 32)
