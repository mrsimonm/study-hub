## Historical and regional geology: what and why | 5 min

:::idea The idea
**Historical geology** asks *what happened, and when*. **Regional geology** asks *what happened here*, in one large region. Both read the Earth's history from its rocks.
:::

| Field | Definition (slides 2–3) |
|---|---|
| **Historical geology** (palaeogeology) | Uses the principles and methods of geology to reconstruct the geological history of Earth |
| **Regional geology** | The geological study of large-scale regions; usually combines several geological disciplines to piece together the history of an area |

### What this lecture covers (slide 5)

1. **Dating techniques**: relative dating and absolute dating
2. **Geological time**
3. **Early Earth evolution**: Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic
4. **Supercontinent cycle**

:::ana Analogy
Relative dating is like knowing that grandma is older than mum, who is older than you. Absolute dating is reading the birth dates on everyone's passport.
:::

## Relative dating: the four principles | 10 min

:::idea The idea
**Relative dating** puts rocks and events in **order** (older or younger) without giving a number. Four simple principles do most of the work.
:::

| Principle | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Superposition** | Sedimentary layers get **younger upwards** |
| **Original horizontality** | Sedimentary layers were originally deposited **horizontally** |
| **Lateral continuity** | Sedimentary layers spread laterally in all directions until they hit the **edge of the basin** |
| **Cross-cutting relationships** | Folds, faults and igneous intrusions that cut across rocks are **younger** than the rocks they deform or cut |

The photo on slide 7 is the **Badlands, USA**: the same flat layers continue on both sides of each valley. Erosion cut the valleys later, but the layers were once continuous (lateral continuity).

The photo on slide 8 shows light and dark **dikes** cutting straight through the rock: by cross-cutting, each dike is younger than the rock it cuts.

:::trap Exam trap
Superposition works for layers that are still the right way up. If a layer is **tilted or folded**, original horizontality tells you it was deformed **after** it was deposited, and cross-cutting tells you the deformation is younger than the layer.
:::

## Relative dating puzzle (slide 9) | 8 min

:::idea The idea
Slide 9 is a cross-section to put in order. Use two principles: **superposition** for the layers, **cross-cutting** for everything that cuts them.
:::

What the diagram shows:

- Three sedimentary layers: **A** (top), **B** (middle), **C** (bottom).
- **D**: an igneous dike that cuts through C, B and A.
- **E**: a fault that cuts all the layers **and offsets the dike D**.

### The answer, oldest to youngest

1. **C** is deposited (lowest layer)
2. **B** is deposited on top
3. **A** is deposited on top
4. **D** intrudes and cuts C, B and A
5. **E** faults everything, including the dike

:::trap Exam trap
The key clue is that the dike is **broken and shifted** along the fault. Whatever is cut is older, so the dike is older than the fault and **E is the youngest**.
:::

:::ana Link to lecture 7
On the right of the fault, layer B sits lower than on the left. The right block is the **hanging wall**, and it moved **down**: a **normal fault**.
:::

## Absolute dating and radioactive decay | 10 min

:::idea The idea
**Absolute dating** gives the precise age in years using **radiometric** techniques. It works because radioactive isotopes decay at a steady, known rate.
:::

**Radioactivity** is the spontaneous decay of radioactive isotopes. It was discovered by **Henri Becquerel** in **1896**.

### Three kinds of radiation

| Type | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alpha decay** | A **helium nucleus** is ejected | U-238 → Th-234 |
| **Beta decay** | A **neutron** breaks down into a proton and an electron; the **electron** is ejected | C-14 → N-14 |
| **Gamma rays** | Accompanying **electromagnetic radiation** | |

:::ana Why the numbers change
Alpha decay loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons, so uranium (92) becomes thorium (90) and the mass drops by 4 (238 → 234). In beta decay a neutron turns into a proton, so carbon (6) becomes nitrogen (7) and the mass stays 14.
:::

### Penetrating power (figure on slide 12)

| Radiation | Stopped by |
|---|---|
| **Alpha** | A sheet of **paper** |
| **Beta** | **Aluminium** |
| **Gamma** | Only weakened by thick **concrete** (or lead) |

The figure also notes that beta decay can release a **positron** (β⁺) instead of an electron.

## Parent, daughter, half-life | 10 min

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Parent isotope** | The original radioactive element |
| **Daughter isotope** | The radioactive decay product |
| **Half-life** | The time for **half of the atoms** to decay |
| **Decay chain** | Parents and daughters keep forming until a **stable** (non-radioactive) daughter is reached, e.g. U-238 → Pb-206 |

### How half-life works

| Half-lives passed | Parent left | Daughter formed |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100 % | 0 % |
| 1 | 50 % | 50 % |
| 2 | 25 % | 75 % |
| 3 | 12.5 % | 87.5 % |

So by measuring the ratio of parent to daughter in a mineral, you can count how many half-lives have passed.

### The dating pairs (slide 14)

| Parent → daughter | Half-life | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Uranium-238 → Lead-206** | **4.5 billion years** | Old events |
| Uranium-235 → Lead-207 | 704 million years | Old events |
| Potassium-40 → Argon-40 | 1.25 billion years | Old events |
| Rubidium-87 → Strontium-87 | 48.8 billion years | Old events |
| **Carbon-14 → Nitrogen-14** | **5,730 years** | **Young** events |

:::trap Exam trap
**Carbon-14 cannot date dinosaurs or old rocks.** Its half-life is only 5,730 years, so after a few tens of thousands of years almost none is left. For old rocks use U-Pb, K-Ar or Rb-Sr.
:::

## Geological time and deep time | 6 min

:::idea The idea
**Geological time** refers to the immense timescales over which geological processes operate. Two thinkers made people accept how long it is.
:::

### James Hutton (Siccar Point, Scotland)

- *"The present is the key to the past."* The processes we see today (erosion, deposition, volcanism) worked the same way in the past.
- *"No vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end."* The Earth is unimaginably old.

### Siccar Point (slide 17)

| Part | What the slide says |
|---|---|
| **Below** the red line | **Vertical** sedimentary rocks with **marine fossils**, deposited in a **deep ocean** about **425 Ma** (Silurian) |
| **The red line** | A cross-cutting **unconformity**: a **hiatus**, a period of **non-deposition or active erosion** |
| **Above** the red line | **Tilted** sedimentary rocks deposited in a **desert** about **345 Ma** (Devonian) |

To explain this, Hutton needed a very long time: the ocean layers were deposited, turned vertical, uplifted and eroded flat, and only then were the desert layers laid on top.

:::skip Don't memorise
The exact wording "The present is the key to the past" was written later (by Archibald Geikie) to sum up Hutton's idea. The slide credits Hutton, so answer **Hutton** in the test.
:::

### Charles Darwin

- *"Endless forms most beautiful…"*
- He **needed deep time to explain the evolution of species**.

## Dividing geological time | 10 min

:::idea The idea
Time itself is split into **geochronological** units. The **rocks** that formed during each unit get a matching **chronostratigraphic** name.
:::

| Geochronology (time) | Chronostratigraphy (rocks) |
|---|---|
| **Eon** | Eonthem |
| **Era** | Erathem |
| **Period** | System |
| **Epoch** | Series |
| **Age** | Stage |

:::ana Analogy
"The Jurassic **Period**" is a span of time. "The Jurassic **System**" is the pile of rocks laid down during it. Same name, one is time, the other is rock.
:::

## Stratigraphy and the key numbers | 8 min

**Stratigraphy** is the study of rock layers and their relationships in space and time.

| Type | Based on | Typical unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronostratigraphy** | Relative geological time represented by rocks | System, Series, Stage |
| **Lithostratigraphy** | Rock type (lithology) | Formation, Member, Group |
| **Biostratigraphy** | Fossil content | Biozone |
| **Geochronology** | Absolute dating | An age, e.g. 341 ± 3 Ma |

### Numbers to know (slide 21)

| Event | When |
|---|---|
| **Age of the Earth** | ~**4.6 Ga** |
| **First life** | **> 3.5 Ga** |
| **Beginning of the Phanerozoic** | ~**540 Ma** |
| Beginning of the Quaternary | ~2 Ma |
| Beginning of the Holocene | ~10 ka |

**Ga** = billions of years ago, **Ma** = millions of years ago, **ka** = thousands of years ago.

:::skip Don't memorise
The exact chart values. The slide 19 chart uses older numbers (Cambrian 542 Ma, Triassic 251 Ma, Paleogene 65.5 Ma). Today's chart gives about 539, 252 and 66 Ma, the Quaternary 2.58 Ma and the Holocene 11,700 years. Learn the rounded slide 21 numbers.
:::

## Early Earth: origin and the Hadean | 8 min

### Origin: the nebular theory

- A **rotating cloud of gas and dust**, ~**4.6 Ga**
- The **centre** became the **Sun**; material in **orbit** became the **planets**
- Slide 23 shows **HL Tauri**, a young star with a protoplanetary disk: the same process seen today

### Hadean eon: 4.6–4 Ga

- A **"lava planet"**
- **Differentiation** into **core, mantle and crust**
- **Cooling** → crust
- **Degassing** → atmosphere
- Likely **liquid water (oceans)** by the end
- **No rocks**, only very rare **zircons** preserved

:::ana Analogy
Differentiation is like a salad dressing left to stand: the heavy part (iron) sinks to the bottom and the light part (silicates) floats to the top.
:::

:::skip Don't memorise
The oldest known Hadean zircons come from the Jack Hills in Western Australia and are about 4.4 billion years old. Not on the slides.
:::

## Archean and Proterozoic | 10 min

### Archean eon: 4–2.5 Ga

- The Earth **continued cooling**
- **First continental crust**
- **First microbial life**: **stromatolites**
- **First photosynthesis**: oxygen starts to rise

Slide 26 is a world map of **Archean cratons**: small cores of very old crust inside today's continents (North America, Greenland, Baltic, Siberia, Africa, India, Australia, Antarctica and others).

**Stromatolites** are layered mounds built by microbial mats. They are among the oldest evidence of life, and a few still grow today (slide 27).

### Proterozoic eon: 2500–540 Ma

- **Stabilization and growth** of continents
- Development of **modern-style plate tectonics** and the **supercontinent cycle**
- **Atmospheric oxygen rise**
- First **eukaryotic cells**
- First **multicellular life** (late-Proterozoic fauna, slide 29)

The map on slide 28 shows how continents grew outwards: small **Archean cratons** at the core, wide **Proterozoic shields** around them, and younger **Phanerozoic orogens** (mountain belts) at the edges. Named shields include the North American, Siberian, African, Indian, South American, Australian and East Antarctic shields.

:::trap Exam trap
Keep the "firsts" apart. **Archean**: first continental crust, first microbes (stromatolites), first photosynthesis. **Proterozoic**: first eukaryotes, first multicellular life, big oxygen rise.
:::

| Eon | Time |
|---|---|
| Hadean | 4.6–4 Ga |
| Archean | 4–2.5 Ga |
| Proterozoic | 2500–540 Ma |
| Phanerozoic | 540 Ma–today |

## Continents: cratons, shields and more | 8 min

:::idea The idea
These words describe the building blocks of continents. They come back in every regional geology lecture.
:::

| Term | Meaning (slide 30) |
|---|---|
| **Craton** | Old (Precambrian), stable core of a continent |
| **Shield** | **Exposed** part of a craton; Precambrian basement at the surface |
| **Platform** | Craton **covered** by younger, mostly sedimentary rocks |
| **Basement** | Older crystalline rocks underlying younger sedimentary cover |
| **Sedimentary cover** | Younger sediments or rocks deposited over the basement |
| **Orogen** | Mountain belt formed by tectonic convergence |
| **Terrane** | Crustal block with a geological history distinct from the surrounding rocks |
| **Basin** | Subsiding area where sediments accumulate |

:::ana Analogy
A craton is an old wooden table. Where you can see the bare wood, it is a **shield**. Where a tablecloth (sediments) lies on top, it is a **platform**. The wood is the **basement**, the tablecloth the **sedimentary cover**.
:::

## The supercontinent cycle | 8 min

:::idea The idea
The **supercontinent cycle** is the **Wilson cycle repeated** (lecture 7): all the continents merge at one point into a supercontinent and then break up again.
:::

| Supercontinent | About |
|---|---|
| **Kenorland** | ~2.7 Ga |
| **Columbia (Nuna)** | ~1.8 Ga |
| **Rodinia** | ~1.0 Ga |
| **Pannotia** | ~600 Ma |
| **Pangea** | ~300 Ma |
| Today | Dispersed continents |
| Future | Probably another supercontinent |

:::ana Memory trick
**K**en **C**ould **R**ide **P**ast **P**angea: Kenorland, Columbia, Rodinia, Pannotia, Pangea.
