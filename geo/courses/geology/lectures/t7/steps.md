## Three fields, and why bother | 6 min

:::idea The idea
Three fields look at the same Earth at different **scales**. This lecture covers the two smaller ones: **structural geology** and **tectonics**.
:::

| Field | Scale | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Earth structure** | Global | Composition and layering (lecture 6) |
| **Tectonics** | Regional to global | Large-scale plate interactions |
| **Structural geology** | Outcrop to regional | Deformational features in rocks |

**Structural geology** is the branch of geology that studies the architecture of the Earth's crust and the processes responsible for its deformation.

### Why bother?

- Reconstructing the **history** of a rock or a region
- **Geomorphology** (how landscapes form)
- **Natural resource exploration**
- **Geohazard assessment**: cracking, earthquakes, rockfalls, landslides, tsunami
- **Building foundations**

:::teach Teacher said
To assess geohazards like earthquakes, rockfalls, landslides and tsunamis, you have to understand structural geology. {rec}
:::

## Stress, strain and deformation | 10 min

:::idea The idea
**Stress** pushes or pulls on a rock. **Strain** is how the rock changes in response. When the stress is bigger than the rock's **internal strength**, the rock deforms.
:::

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Stress** | The force exerted per unit area |
| **Strain** | The physical change that results in response to that force |
| **Deformation** | Any change in a rock's shape, position or orientation due to stress |
| **Internal strength** | The ability of a rock to resist deformation under stress |

Internal strength is controlled by the rock's **composition**, **temperature**, **pressure** and **strain rate**.

:::teach Teacher said
Deformation is wider than strain. Strain is the change *inside* the material. Deformation also includes the rock **moving** or **rotating**, for example a whole rock layer being tilted. {rec}
:::

:::trap Exam trap
The key sentence from the slide: when applied **stress** is greater than the **internal strength** of a rock, **strain** results in the form of deformation of the rock.
:::

## Types of stress and strain | 10 min

### Three types of stress

| Stress | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Tensional** | Pulling in opposite directions |
| **Compressional** | Pushing together |
| **Shear** | Transverse forces: blocks slide past each other |

### Three types of strain

| Strain | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Elastic** | Reversible: the rock goes back to its shape when the stress is released |
| **Ductile** | The material changes its shape permanently (it bends or flows) |
| **Brittle** | The material fractures (it breaks) |

:::ana Analogy
A plastic ruler bends and springs back: elastic. Warm modelling clay bends and stays bent: ductile. A cold chocolate bar snaps: brittle.
:::

:::teach Teacher said
First the rock deforms elastically. Once the stress passes its **peak (internal) strength**, the deformation becomes permanent: either brittle or ductile. {rec}
:::

### What decides brittle or ductile?

- **High temperature** → ductile
- **High pressure** → ductile
- **High strain rate** (fast deformation) → brittle
- **Fluids** and **composition** also play a role

:::teach Teacher said
A high strain rate is not an absolute rule. Deep down, with high pressure and temperature, a rock is still ductile even when it deforms fast. The strain rate matters most near the transition between brittle and ductile conditions. {rec}
:::

## Brittle deformation: joints, faults, veins | 12 min

:::idea The idea
Brittle deformation **breaks** the rock. The question that sorts the three structures: did the two sides **move**, and is the crack **filled**?
:::

| Structure | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Joint** | A planar fracture along which there has been **no visible displacement** |
| **Fault** | A planar fracture along which **displacement has occurred** |
| **Vein** | A fracture filling: minerals **precipitated from fluids** in open cracks |

:::trap Exam trap
Joint and fault are both fractures. The only difference is **displacement**: layers that matched before are shifted along a fault, but not along a joint.
:::

:::teach Teacher said
Veins mostly contain **quartz** or **calcite**, and they can hold **ores** such as gold. A vein can form in a joint or in a fault. {rec}
:::

### Typical conditions of brittle deformation

| Factor | Brittle |
|---|---|
| Depth | **0–15 km** |
| Temperature | **below about 300 °C** |
| Pressure | **Low** confining pressure |
| Strain rate | **Higher** strain rate favours brittle |
| Result | Fracturing, faulting |

:::teach Teacher said
**Confining pressure** is the lithostatic pressure, which acts equally in every direction. {rec}
:::

## Describing faults and the four fault types | 12 min

### Parts of a fault

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Fault plane** | The plane of displacement |
| **Hanging wall** | The **upper** block |
| **Foot wall** | The **lower** block |
| **Fault line** | Where the fault plane meets the surface |
| **Fault scarp** | A steep slope or cliff on the surface created by a fault |

:::teach Teacher said
The names come from old miners. Ores often formed along faults, so miners dug along them. They **hung** their lantern on the upper block (the hanging wall) and stood with their **feet** on the lower block (the foot wall). {rec}
:::

### Four types of fault

| Fault | Stress | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | Extension (tensional) | Hanging wall moves **down** |
| **Reverse** | Compression | Hanging wall moves **up** |
| **Thrust** | Compression | Hanging wall moves up, but at a **low angle** |
| **Strike-slip** | Shear | Blocks move **sideways** (laterally) past each other |

:::ana Analogy
Pull a stack of books apart and the top block slides **down** into the gap: normal. Squeeze it together and the top block rides **up**: reverse.
:::

:::skip Not tested
The exact angle that separates a reverse fault from a thrust fault. The teacher said you don't need it: a thrust is just a reverse fault that is almost horizontal. {rec}
:::

## Ductile deformation: folds and foliation | 12 min

:::idea The idea
Ductile deformation **bends** the rock instead of breaking it. The two main results are **folds** and **foliation**.
:::

| Structure | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Fold** | Curved or bent layers formed by ductile bending |
| **Foliation** | A planar fabric from aligned platy or elongate minerals (e.g. micas), or compositional layering, formed during ductile deformation |

### Two types of fold

| Fold | Shape |
|---|---|
| **Anticline** | An arch (∩) |
| **Syncline** | A U-shape, like a valley |

:::trap Exam trap
The slide labels the **syncline** "convex (U)" and the **anticline** "concave (∩)". Many textbooks use these two words the other way round, so learn the **shapes**: anticline = arch ∩, syncline = U. If a question uses the words, go with the slide.
:::

:::teach Teacher said
See real folds at **Barrandovská skála** in Prague, the Barrandov cliff just south of Smíchov, along the cycling path. {rec}
:::

### Types of foliation (you know them from lecture 5)

| Foliation | Look | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Slaty cleavage** | Fine-grained, planar | Low |
| **Schistosity** | Wavy planar, mica alignment | Medium |
| **Gneissic banding** | Compositional segregation | High |

### Typical conditions of ductile deformation

| Factor | Ductile |
|---|---|
| Depth | **more than about 10 km** |
| Temperature | **above about 300 °C** |
| Pressure | **High** confining pressure |
| Strain rate | **Lower** strain rate favours ductile |
| Result | Folding, crystal-plastic deformation |

:::trap Exam trap
The two depth ranges overlap: brittle is **0–15 km**, ductile is **deeper than about 10 km**. Between 10 and 15 km, temperature and strain rate decide.
:::

:::teach Teacher said
When shear stress acts over large regions, the result is a **shear zone**. Brittle and ductile deformation often occur together in the same place. {rec}
:::

### Activity: which deformation is which? (slide 21)

The same three stresses give different structures near the surface (brittle) and deeper down (ductile):

| Stress | Shallow: brittle fracture | Deeper: ductile flow |
|---|---|---|
| **Compression** | **Reverse fault** | **Folds** |
| **Tension** | **Normal fault** | Stretching (thinning) of the layers |
| **Shear** | **Strike-slip fault** | **Shear zone** |

:::teach Teacher said
The slide leaves the boxes empty. The teacher filled in the two ductile answers for tension and shear: tension at depth simply **stretches** the layers, and shear over a large region makes a **shear zone**. Brittle and ductile structures often occur together in the same place. {rec}
:::

## Structures shape landscapes | 6 min

**Geomorphology** is the science of how Earth's surface landscapes are formed and changed by natural processes. Structures underground control what the surface looks like.

### Ridge and Valley section of the Appalachians (eastern USA)

- Long parallel ridges run for hundreds of kilometres, and rivers have to bend to follow them.
- A cross-section shows the rock layers are **folded**.

:::teach Teacher said
The ridges formed in the **synclines**, not the anticlines. The top layers there were harder and resisted erosion, while softer layers below wore away. {rec}
:::

### Great Rift Valley in East Africa

- Africa is splitting apart, which forms a long **rift valley**.
- Water fills parts of the valley as long, narrow lakes such as **Lake Malawi**. {rec}

## Plates and what moves them | 8 min

:::idea The idea
The **lithosphere** is broken into a mosaic of **rigid plates**. Each plate is rigid inside; deformation concentrates along the **plate boundaries**.
:::

- Plates move at **a few cm per year**: "Earth dynamics at fingernail speed".
- The motion is measured using **GPS**.

### How is it possible? Mantle convection

1. Mantle rocks are heated near the **core**.
2. Heating increases their volume and decreases their density, so they **rise**.
3. Near the surface they **cool** and **sink** again.

The rigid lithospheric plates "float" on the viscous **asthenosphere**.

:::ana Analogy
A pot of boiling water: heated at the bottom, cooled at the surface, so the water keeps circling. The teacher used this picture. {rec}
:::

### Three kinds of plate boundary

Plate boundaries are the sites of **deformation, earthquakes and magmatism**. Each type matches one type of stress:

| Boundary | Plates | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Divergent** | Move apart | Crustal **extension** (tension) |
| **Convergent** | Converge, collide | Crustal **compression** |
| **Transform** | Slide past each other | **Horizontal shear** |

:::teach Teacher said
Plate boundaries are the most interesting places for geologists: **most rocks form there**, and so do many **ores**. They also shape landscapes (geomorphology) and are where **geohazards** and **volcanism** happen. {rec}
:::

## Divergent boundaries | 10 min

At a **divergent** boundary two plates move apart.

- Main stress regime: **tensional**
- The upper mantle **melts** → magma cools and crystallises → **mafic rocks** (**gabbro**, **basalt**)
- Formation of **normal faults**, **mid-ocean ridges** and **rift valleys**

| Where | What forms | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Continental crust** | Continental rift: **rift valleys** | East African Rift; **Eger Rift** (Czechia, 18 Ma) |
| **Oceanic crust** | **Mid-ocean ridge**: new oceanic crust | Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East Pacific Rise |

:::teach Teacher said
The crust is thinnest here, so the asthenosphere rises close to the surface and partly melts. Below the surface the magma becomes **gabbro**, on the surface **basalt**. {rec}
:::

:::teach Teacher said
How an ocean opens, step by step: a **rift valley** (East Africa) → a young, narrow sea with a ridge in the middle (the **Red Sea**) → a wide **ocean basin** with a clear mid-ocean ridge (the **Atlantic**). {rec}
:::

## Convergent boundaries and the subduction factory | 15 min

At a **convergent** boundary two plates move toward each other. There are three types:

| Plates meeting | What happens | What forms |
|---|---|---|
| **Oceanic × oceanic** | **Intra-oceanic subduction** | **Island arc** |
| **Oceanic × continental** | **Ocean–continent (Andean-type) subduction** | **Continental arc** (accretionary orogen) |
| **Continental × continental** | **Continental collision** | **Orogenic (mountain) belts** |

**Subduction** is when one plate is dragged down beneath the other, into the mantle.

:::teach Teacher said
A subducted plate can sink very deep, in some cases **down to the core**. Geologists can follow these sunken plates inside the Earth and read the history of subduction millions of years back. {rec}
:::

:::teach Teacher said
The **oceanic** plate always subducts beneath the **continental** plate, because oceanic plate is denser (heavier) and continental plate is more buoyant. There are rare exceptions. {rec}
:::

### Both kinds of subduction arc

- Dominantly **andesitic volcanism**
- **Dioritic to granodioritic plutonism** (intermediate magma)
- Examples from class: the islands from southern Japan to Taiwan (island arc), the **Andes** (continental arc). {rec}
- In continental arcs the intermediate magma forms **large plutons (batholiths)**. {rec}

### The subduction factory

1. **Magmatism**: large plutons and volcanoes, dominantly **intermediate** composition
2. **Metamorphism**: **paired metamorphism**
   - **High P/T** (**blueschist–eclogite**) in the subducting plate
   - **Medium P/T** (**greenschist–amphibolite–granulite**) in the upper plate
3. **Earthquakes**: they follow the subducting plate

:::teach Teacher said
Why earthquakes happen: the plates build up stress as **elastic strain**. When the rock can no longer hold it, the strain is released in a **sudden rupture**, a sudden movement along the plate. {rec}
:::

:::teach Teacher said
The subducting oceanic plate is mostly basalt and gabbro. That is why blueschist and eclogite are typical metabasaltic rocks. In Japan you can trace the sinking plate by plotting earthquake depths: shallow near the trench, deeper and deeper away from it. {rec}
:::

:::skip Note
The teacher said "epicentres" for the points inside the Earth. Strictly, the point inside the Earth where an earthquake starts is the **focus** (hypocentre), and the **epicentre** is the point on the surface above it.
:::

### Continental collision

- Formation of **orogenic belts** (mountain belts), e.g. the **Alps** and the **Himalayas** {rec}
- **Thrusting** and **folding**
- **Regional metamorphism**
- **Plutonic** magmatism (not much volcanic)
- **Earthquakes** (for example in Nepal) {rec}

## Transform boundaries and hotspots | 6 min

At a **transform** boundary, continental or oceanic plates **slide past each other**.

- **Strike-slip faults**
- **No significant metamorphism**
- **No significant magmatism**
- **Earthquakes!**

:::teach Teacher said
Examples: the **San Andreas Fault** in California, and the big earthquake in **Turkey** a few years ago. Along the San Andreas Fault a **small mountain range** formed, from a little thrusting and folding along the fault. {rec}
:::

:::teach Teacher said
**Hotspots** such as **Hawaii** are not at a plate boundary. A **mantle plume** brings magma up from deep in the mantle, so volcanoes form in the middle of a plate. {rec}
:::

## The Wilson cycle | 8 min

:::idea The idea
The **Wilson cycle** describes the repeated **opening and closing of ocean basins** through plate tectonic processes: the life cycle of plate tectonics.
:::

1. **Continental rift**: a rift valley forms in a continent.
2. **Young ocean**: the plates keep spreading; a narrow sea opens.
3. **Ocean basin**: a wide ocean with a spreading mid-ocean ridge.
4. **Subduction** starts: intra-oceanic, then beneath a continent.
5. **Continental collision**: the ocean closes and a mountain belt (orogen) forms.
6. **Erosion** of the orogen, and later a new **rift** can open inside it. The cycle starts again.

It takes **hundreds of millions of years**.

:::teach Teacher said
The fossil-rich **Prague basin** rocks (about 400 million years old) are ocean sediments that did not subduct but were squeezed between two continents just before they collided. {rec}
:::
