## The Big Five | 3 min
A **mass extinction** = at least **75 %** of species lost in a geologically short time. The **Big Five**, in order:

1. End-Ordovician
2. Late Devonian
3. End-Permian
4. End-Triassic
5. End-Cretaceous

## Rates vs totals | 6 min
**Background extinction rate (BGER)**: the natural pace, from the fossil record (marine invertebrates, mammal ranges). Estimates are 0.1–1 E/MSY, so Ceballos used a conservative **2 E/MSY** ≈ 1 extinction per 10,000 species per 100 years.

Ceballos et al. used two scenarios: **highly conservative** (only officially extinct species) and **conservative** (also extinct in the wild and possibly extinct).

:::teach Two definitions, two answers
| | Ceballos et al. 2015 | Wiens & Saban 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Defines mass extinction by | **rate** vs background | **total loss** (75 %) |
| Answer | yes, we are entering it | not by this measure |
:::

**For**: modern rates vastly exceed background; vertebrate losses of the last 100 years would naturally take 800–1,000 years.
**Against**: total loss is still very low; historical extinctions are biased toward islands; invertebrates are omitted; some assumptions are unrealistic.

## Human impact and extinction debt | 5 min
Habitat loss and fragmentation endanger **85 %** of IUCN Red List species. Agriculture, deforestation, pollution and poaching are named drivers.

**Extinction debt**: future extinctions already committed by past damage. The lag can be years, decades or centuries, and it is a **conservation window**.
- White-cheeked spider monkey (Brazilian Amazon): farmland fragmentation, lower breeding, more food competition.
- *Rhabdothamnus solandri* (New Zealand shrub): several bird pollinators (e.g. stitchbird, bellbird) disappeared locally around 1870.
