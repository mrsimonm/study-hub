## Life-history traits | 4 min
**Life-history traits** describe how an organism spends its life: growth, **age and size at maturity**, **parity** (breeding once like a sockeye salmon or many times like a rabbit), **parental investment**, number and size of offspring, and **lifespan**.

A **life-history strategy** is a combination of these traits. Only limited combinations exist, because of trade-offs.

## The slow–fast continuum | 5 min
The main axis of life-history variation, defined by the trade-off between allocating time to **reproduction and development** versus **survival**.

| Fast | Slow |
|---|---|
| fast development, early maturation | slow development, late maturation |
| early reproduction, many offspring | late reproduction, few offspring |
| short lifespan, high mortality | long lifespan, low mortality |
| fruit fly: 10–12 day life cycle | Przewalski's horse: mature at 4–5, lives ~20 years |

**Pace-of-life syndromes** extend this to behaviour and physiology: fast-growing, bold, high-metabolism individuals vs slow-growing, shy, low-metabolism ones.

## Is one axis enough? | 5 min
:::idea Group 1's answer
The fast–slow continuum is the **dominant axis** in tetrapods, and gives a broad picture for vascular plants and corals. But there are few studies in invertebrates (except insects), and **many organisms fall outside its predictions**. So **multiple axes** are needed.
:::

Examples of extra axes:
- **Reproductive and developmental patterns**.
- **Stature–recruitment axis**: *Psychotria horizontalis* (high recruitment, low juvenile mortality) vs the kapok tree *Ceiba pentandra* (low recruitment, high juvenile mortality).
- **Altricial vs precocial** offspring.

Papers: classic **Stearns (1977)**, recent **Stott et al. (2024)**, "Life histories are not just fast or slow".
