## Network vocabulary | 5 min
- **Keystone species**: a disproportionately large effect compared with its biomass. Removing it can cause cascades or regime shifts.
- **Interaction strength**: how strongly one species changes another's survival, abundance or behaviour.
- **Network structure**:
  - **Degree**: number of links. High-degree species are **hubs**.
  - **Modularity**: clusters that interact more inside than between.
  - **Motifs**: small link patterns that recur more than chance.

## Lake Nakuru as an example | 4 min
| Level | Who |
|---|---|
| Producers | cyanobacteria, mainly *Spirulina* (Arthrospira) |
| Primary consumers | **lesser flamingo** (filter feeder, dominant by biomass), soda tilapia, copepods, chironomid larvae |
| Secondary consumers | **great white pelican** (eats soda tilapia), great cormorant, greater flamingo, African fish eagle |
| Higher consumers | birds of prey, marabou storks |

The terrestrial web (giraffe, zebra, buffalo, rhino; lion, leopard, python) connects to the lake through nutrients and animal movement.

## Complexity, stability and rewiring | 5 min
:::idea Does complexity build biodiversity?
Many feeding links, **alternative prey routes** (diet switching) and varied niches support **resilience**. But highly complex webs are **not automatically stable**.
:::

**Network rewiring** (Bartley et al. 2019): links weaken, disappear or form over time. Drivers are natural (seasons, range shifts, evolution) or human (land use, **species introductions**, climate change). Rewiring can buffer a system, or spread disturbances and create risky new links.

Invasive species rewire webs through resource competition, direct predation, indirect effects and network disruption.
