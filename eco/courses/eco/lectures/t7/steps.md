## The pattern | 4 min
**Macroecology** studies organisms and environment at large scales; **biogeography** studies where organisms live and why.

The **latitudinal diversity gradient (LDG)**: species richness rises from the poles to the equator. It holds across most groups (vertebrates, invertebrates, plants).

Guiding question: *why does biodiversity peak in the tropics and fall toward the poles, and does that hold for every dimension?*

## Dimensions and scales | 5 min
| Dimension | Plain words |
|---|---|
| **Taxonomic** | counting species (richness, evenness) |
| **Functional** | counting jobs: traits and roles (diet, body size, dispersal) |
| **Phylogenetic** | measuring shared evolutionary history |

Spatial scales: **point** (one sample) → **alpha** (one community) → **beta** (difference between communities) → **gamma** (many communities).

## Why does it happen? | 5 min
No single explanation. Group 5's order of priority, after Brodie & Mannion (2023):

1. **Climate & energy**: the main driver (sunlight, temperature, precipitation, seasonality).
2. **Habitat area**: more area → more niches.
3. **Evolutionary history**: **diversification** = speciation − extinction.
4. **Biotic factors**: competition, predation, pollination (local filtering).

History: gradients are **sharper in cold periods**; in hot climates the tropical zone is bigger. **Tropical conservatism**: most taxa evolved in tropical conditions and stayed adapted to them. Fossils support gradients through Earth's history, but fossil records can be biased.

## Exceptions and uses | 3 min
- **Parasites** don't seem to follow the LDG.
- **Aquatic flora**: diversity peaks in temperate zones.
- **Secondary marine mammals and birds**: the gradient reverses (more diversity toward the poles).

Uses: identifying areas to protect, predicting where invasive species spread, modelling future biodiversity.
